Observing the Night Sky

You do not need expensive equipment to enjoy astronomy. Patience, a dark place and a little
preparation matter more than magnification.

Light pollution is the biggest obstacle. Drive away from city lights if you can and check a light
pollution map for dark sites nearby. Your eyes need about twenty to thirty minutes to adapt to the
dark; use a red flashlight to read star charts, because white light resets this adaptation at once.

Binoculars are an excellent first instrument. A pair labelled 7x50 or 10x50 shows the craters of the
Moon, the four large moons of Jupiter and countless star clusters. Rest your elbows on a car roof or
use a tripod to keep the view steady.

When choosing a first telescope, aperture – the diameter of the main lens or mirror – matters more
than magnification. A Dobsonian reflector with a 150 or 200 mm mirror offers the most aperture for
the money and is simple to set up. Be suspicious of small telescopes advertised with huge
magnifications.

Planets are easy targets because they are bright even from the city. Saturn's rings are visible at
around 40x magnification, and Jupiter's cloud belts appear in a small telescope on a steady night.
Check an astronomy app or almanac to see which planets are above the horizon this month. The best
time to observe the Moon is not at full moon but near the first quarter, when shadows along the
terminator reveal mountains and craters.
