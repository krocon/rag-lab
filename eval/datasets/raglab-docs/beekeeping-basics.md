# Beekeeping Basics

Keeping honey bees is rewarding but seasonal work. A colony is a single organism of one queen,
thousands of female workers and, in summer, a few hundred male drones.

## Setting up a hive

Most beginners use a Langstroth hive: a stack of boxes with removable wooden frames. The lower
boxes, called brood boxes, hold the queen's eggs and larvae; the upper boxes, called supers, store
surplus honey. Place the hive in a sunny spot sheltered from wind, with the entrance facing away
from footpaths, and give the bees a nearby water source so they do not visit the neighbours' pool.

## Inspections and swarming

Inspect the colony every seven to ten days during spring. Look for eggs, which prove the queen was
present recently, and for queen cells along the bottom of frames. Many queen cells in May usually
mean the colony is preparing to swarm: the old queen leaves with about half of the workers. Giving
the colony more space or making an artificial split reduces the urge to swarm.

## Varroa mites

The varroa mite is a parasite that feeds on bees and spreads viruses. Every colony has some mites,
so beekeepers count them, for example with a sugar roll or a sticky board under a mesh floor. When
the count passes a threshold, treat the colony – common options are oxalic acid in winter, when
there is no brood, and formic acid in late summer. Untreated colonies often collapse within two
years.

## Harvesting honey

Harvest only capped honey: bees seal a cell with wax once the moisture content is low enough. Clear
the bees from the super with a bee escape board, uncap the frames with a heated knife and spin them
in an extractor. Strain the honey and store it in clean jars. Always leave the colony enough honey
to survive the winter, roughly 15 to 20 kg in a temperate climate.
